---
title: Everyone has a Wand, so why didn't Everyone build the Marauder's Map?
date: '2026-10-27'
spoiler: Lately, I've been seeing people claim that software is dead.
---
![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrITaRXIAAJDSY.jpg)

Lately, I've been seeing people claim that software is dead.

The argument goes something like this: AI agents are becoming so good at writing code that eventually everyone will be able to generate whatever software they need. Instead of downloading an application, subscribing to a service, or buying a piece of software, you'll simply tell an agent what you want, and it will create it.

If anyone can generate software on demand, why would software itself remain valuable?

At first this sounds plausible. And I suspect part of it is true. A lot of software that people currently pay for might eventually become something an AI agent can generate in a few seconds.

But there's something strange about the conclusion.

To see why, consider the world of Harry Potter.

In the wizarding world, almost everyone has access to magic. People can make objects move, repair broken things, transform materials, communicate over long distances, and even teleport.

Obviously, not every wizard can perform every spell. Magic requires knowledge and practice. But compared with our world, the average wizard has access to an extraordinary set of capabilities.

A wand is something like the ultimate general-purpose tool.

And yet, the wizarding world is full of inventions.

This is what makes it interesting.

Take the Weasley family's home.

![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrC9qBXoAAEGLQ.jpg)

The Burrow is filled with magical automation. Dishes wash themselves. Pots stir themselves. Knitting needles work without anyone holding them.

There are moving photographs, enchanted objects, and all sorts of conveniences that would seem extraordinary to us but are perfectly ordinary to wizards.

Even writing can be automated. Enchanted quills can write on their own. Rita Skeeter has a Quick-Quotes Quill that turns conversations into articles, though the results aren't especially reliable.

This is remarkably similar to what we're beginning to do with AI.

We have agents that write documents, generate code, manipulate files, and carry out tasks on our computers. We're trying to build systems that can understand requests and perform complicated sequences of actions without being told every individual step.

The wizarding world already has a version of this.

But one of the most interesting objects in the Weasleys' house is a clock.

![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrDe6uWMAAL_GH.jpg)

Unlike an ordinary clock, it doesn't tell time. Instead, each hand represents a member of the family and points to their current situation: home, work, school, travelling, or even mortal peril.

Think about how peculiar this invention is.

In a world where people can perform magic, someone decided to create a clock that answers a question clocks weren't originally designed to answer.

An ordinary clock tells you what time it is.

The Weasleys' clock tells you whether your family is safe.

It's not merely an automated version of an ordinary clock. It's a different kind of object.

Someone had to imagine it.

And that's the interesting part. Magic makes the clock possible, but magic doesn't explain why someone thought to make it.

You could give a thousand wizards access to the same spells, and most of them probably wouldn't think of building that particular clock.

They might build other things. Some might create tools for gardening. Others might improve transportation. Someone might find a better way to organize books.

The range of things they could create would be enormous.

But access to a capability doesn't tell you what to do with it.

This seems to be one of the things missing from the argument that software is dead.

People are confusing the ability to generate software with the end of the need to invent software.

These are very different things.

There's another example in Harry Potter that illustrates why.

In *The Goblet of Fire*, Dumbledore creates an Age Line around the Goblet to prevent students younger than seventeen from entering the Triwizard Tournament.

![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrEX3MXoAA0EKQ.jpg)

Fred and George Weasley decide to defeat it using an Ageing Potion.

Their reasoning is simple. If the protection prevents people below a certain age from entering, why not magically become older?

Hermione is skeptical. In the film, she points out that Dumbledore is too clever to be fooled by something so elementary.

The twins try anyway and end up with enormous white beards.

![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrEm9TWsAAqVWX.jpg)

What I find interesting about this scene is that everyone involved has access to magic.

Fred and George know spells and potions. They can modify things about themselves. They're capable of inventing surprisingly sophisticated magical products.

And yet, that doesn't mean they can easily defeat Dumbledore's protection.

Dumbledore has created something that requires more than knowing which spell to cast.

He has designed a system.

And the details of that system matter.

In fact, the tournament's protections aren't perfect. Barty Crouch Jr. eventually tricks the Goblet into accepting Harry's name by manipulating the Goblet itself rather than simply crossing the Age Line.

This is almost exactly how sophisticated software systems behave. A component may correctly enforce one rule while the larger system remains vulnerable through some unexpected interaction.

The important thing isn't merely whether you can create a magical barrier.

It's understanding what the barrier is supposed to protect, how it fits into the rest of the system, and what assumptions it depends on.

Now imagine that every wizard had access to an AI agent that could generate enchantments.

Would that make Dumbledore's protections unnecessary?

Probably not. It might make them easier to create, and it might help attackers discover weaknesses. But the need for carefully designed protections wouldn't disappear.

If anything, a world in which everyone could generate powerful enchantments might require more sophisticated protections than before.

The same could happen with software.

If everyone can generate applications, we'll probably generate far more of them. And those applications will need to communicate, share information, enforce permissions, manage identities, and operate reliably.

Being able to generate each component doesn't eliminate the need for systems that make those components useful together.

It may create more opportunities for such systems.

But there's an even better example.

The Marauder's Map.

![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrFKQWWwAAu8OH.jpg)

I think this is one of the most interesting inventions in the entire Harry Potter universe.

It's a piece of parchment that displays the Hogwarts castle, including its hidden passages and the location of every person moving through it.

The map shows people even when they're disguised or hiding beneath an Invisibility Cloak.

It's effectively a real-time tracking system with an interface so simple that a schoolchild can use it.

And it was invented by four students.

James Potter, Sirius Black, Remus Lupin, and Peter Pettigrew.

Think about that for a moment.

Hogwarts had existed for centuries. Thousands of witches and wizards had studied there. Some of the most brilliant magical minds in history had walked its corridors.

Presumably, many of them knew more magic than four teenage boys.

Yet it was those four students who created the Marauder's Map.

Why?

This is the question I'd like people who say software is dead to think about.

Why didn't everyone have a Marauder's Map?

After all, everyone had a wand.

There were professors with much greater magical knowledge than the Marauders. There were adults who had spent their entire lives studying enchantments.

And yet the map wasn't an ordinary thing.

Perhaps someone else could have created something similar. Perhaps the Ministry possessed even more sophisticated tracking magic.

But none of that changes the essential fact that four students invented a useful and unusual magical product.

I suspect the explanation has something to do with how they experienced Hogwarts.

The Marauders were unusually curious about the castle. They spent years exploring its corridors, finding secret passages, and learning things most students never discovered.

They also had a particular problem they wanted to solve: moving around Hogwarts without getting caught.

They understood the problem because they lived with it.

And eventually they created a tool that reflected that understanding.

The map wasn't simply a demonstration of advanced magic. It was the result of looking at a familiar environment in an unusual way.

That's what inventions often are.

There are thousands of things we could build with the technology we already possess. The difficult part is frequently noticing which of them would be useful.

Making the implementation easier wouldn't have eliminated the need for that one idea.

It might have made it possible to try more ideas.

And this is where I think the predictions about software's death get the future backward.

Suppose AI agents become so capable that anyone can describe an application and receive a working implementation.

What happens next?

One possibility is that everyone generates exactly the software they need, and nobody needs to buy software anymore.

But I suspect something else will happen too.

People will begin inventing things they wouldn't previously have considered possible.

When the cost of implementing an idea falls, the set of ideas worth trying becomes much larger.

A student who wants to experiment with a new kind of knowledge-management tool won't need to spend months learning how to build its infrastructure.

A scientist could create specialized software for a problem that only a few hundred people in the world understand.

A small group of developers could build tools that previously required a large company.

And someone might discover an entirely new way of interacting with computers.

Something as unexpected as a clock that doesn't tell time.

Or a map that shows people moving through a castle.

I think Fred and George Weasley offer another interesting example.

They lived in a world where magical abilities were widely available, yet they built a successful business selling magical products.

Weasleys' Wizard Wheezes wasn't successful because the twins had exclusive access to magic.

![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HVrGRgBXAAIvggP.jpg)

They created products that other people wanted.

Extendable Ears allowed people to listen to conversations from a distance. Skiving Snackboxes helped students escape lessons by temporarily making them appear sick. Their magical fireworks behaved in ways ordinary fireworks couldn't.

Each product was a particular combination of magical capabilities designed around a particular desire.

And people bought them.

Why didn't customers simply make these things themselves?

Some probably could have, given enough knowledge and effort.

But being able to create something in principle doesn't mean creating it yourself is the best use of your time.

You might lack the idea, the expertise, the patience, or the desire to experiment until it works properly.

You might simply prefer to use something another person has already made.

This is true even when the tools required to create it are widely available.

Everyone can cook, at least in principle. Restaurants still exist.

Many people can take photographs. They still appreciate the work of exceptional photographers.

These comparisons aren't perfect. AI may reduce the cost of creating software much more dramatically than previous technologies reduced the cost of cooking or photography.

But that makes the question more interesting, not less.

How much of what people value in software comes from the effort of producing it, and how much comes from everything else?

Consider a software system that has accumulated years of knowledge about a specialized domain.

An agent might be able to reproduce its visible features. But reproducing the understanding embedded in its behavior may be much harder, especially if that understanding was acquired through years of mistakes, feedback, and interaction with real users.

In some cases, agents will be able to acquire that knowledge too.

But in others, there will still be value in using something that someone has already thought about carefully.

There's a tendency to equate software with code.

If AI can generate code, the argument goes, then software becomes free.

But code is only one representation of software.

The interesting part of software is often the model of the world that it embodies.

A good piece of software captures something about a problem which information matters, which actions should be possible, which mistakes should be prevented, and how different things relate to one another.

And perhaps AI agents will change the way those models are implemented and used. Instead of buying fixed applications, we may increasingly interact with systems that adapt themselves to our needs, generate interfaces dynamically, and create temporary tools for particular tasks.

Some traditional software products will probably disappear.

Others will become much less valuable.

There may be entire categories of applications that no longer make sense as standalone businesses.

But I don't see why that implies the end of software.

The form changes. The possibilities expand.

What interests me most isn't the idea that we'll use AI to generate the same applications we've been building for the last twenty years.

It's that we may finally be able to create entirely different kinds of applications.

For a long time, software has been shaped by the difficulty of building it.

We use forms, buttons, tables, and dashboards partly because they're useful, but also because they're familiar and comparatively straightforward to implement.

What would software look like if implementation stopped being the main constraint?

Maybe we'd build tools that continuously reorganize themselves around what we're trying to accomplish.

And perhaps many of the most interesting ideas haven't been imagined yet.

That's what makes the Harry Potter analogy useful.

Magic is everywhere in that world, but the existence of magic doesn't make magical inventions uninteresting.

Quite the opposite.

Because so much is possible, there are extraordinary opportunities for people who notice unusual problems or imagine unusual solutions.

The Marauders could have used their wands to play tricks on other students, and they often did. But they also created a map unlike anything most people at Hogwarts had seen.

Fred and George could have spent their lives using ordinary spells. Instead, they created an entire category of products around their interests.

And someone, somewhere, thought it would be useful to build a clock that tells a mother whether her children are safe.

None of these inventions required magic.

They required ideas.

If AI agents eventually give everyone the equivalent of a magical wand, I don't think that will make software disappear.

It will mean that we have more freedom to decide what software should be.

And I suspect we'll discover that there are far more things worth building than we previously imagined.

After all, everyone at Hogwarts had a wand.

But not everyone thought of making the Marauder's Map.
