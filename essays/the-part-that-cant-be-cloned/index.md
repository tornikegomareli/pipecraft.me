---
title: The part of Software which can't be cloned
date: '2026-10-11'
spoiler: If software becomes increasingly easy to copy, what exactly are we competing on anymore?
---
![](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUW4QAPWAAAemac.jpg)

I've been thinking about what happens to software when anyone can build almost anything.

Not long ago, replicating an existing application required a considerable amount of engineering. You had to understand how it worked, figure out its architecture, reproduce its interface, and spend weeks or months implementing the details.

Today, I can record a video of an application, extract a few frames, give them to an AI agent, and ask it to build something similar.

And quite often, it will.

It might not reproduce everything perfectly. It might miss some interactions, misunderstand a few details, or build something that only resembles the original on the surface. But the important thing is that it can produce something recognizable, often in a fraction of the time.

This raises an interesting question.

> If software becomes increasingly easy to copy, what exactly are we competing on anymore?

At first, the answer seems obvious. If someone can reproduce your product in a weekend, perhaps your product has lost its value.

But I'm not convinced that's true.

Consider restaurants.

Anyone can walk into a successful restaurant, order its most popular dish, study the ingredients, and attempt to recreate it. A talented chef might even reproduce the taste almost perfectly.

But that doesn't mean they've recreated the restaurant.

The restaurant isn't just the dish you ate on Saturday night. It's the fact that you can return three months later and expect the same quality. It's the kitchen operating consistently, the staff knowing what they're doing, and someone taking responsibility when something goes wrong.

You can reproduce a meal. Reproducing the institution behind it is a different problem.

I think software is becoming similar.

You can copy an interface. You can copy features. You can even copy the little animations and interactions that make a product feel special.

But can you copy what happens afterward?

Will you still be fixing bugs six months from now?

Will you maintain compatibility when the operating system changes?

Will you answer users when something breaks?

Will you spend an entire weekend investigating a problem that affects only a handful of customers?

Will you keep improving the product after the excitement of building it has disappeared?

And, perhaps most importantly, will you still care?

These questions sound less exciting than generating an application from a prompt. They are also much harder to answer.

Because building something and being responsible for something are two very different things.

The first has a finish line. The second doesn't.

This reminds me of the market for mechanical watches. There are countless replicas of expensive watches, some remarkably close to the originals. Yet people continue to value the genuine ones.

Part of that is certainly branding, status, history, and scarcity. We shouldn't pretend otherwise.

But there's something else worth noticing - the original manufacturer exists beyond the moment of purchase. There is a company behind the object, with expertise, servicing, replacement parts, and a reputation built over decades.

The replica copies the object. It doesn't necessarily reproduce everything that surrounds the object.

Of course, software is different from a luxury watch. Most people don't buy a text editor or a productivity app for its heritage. They buy it because it solves a problem.

But when that software becomes part of their everyday life, another consideration appears.

They need to know it will keep working.

And that kind of confidence isn't something you can generate in a single prompt.

It has to be earned over time.

I think we're entering a strange period in software development. We're becoming extraordinarily good at starting things. Every interesting idea can become a prototype. 

That's wonderful in many ways. More people can create, experiment, and compete.

But I suspect it will also produce an enormous amount of abandoned software.

Beautiful applications with no second release.

Promising tools whose developers lose interest after two weeks.

Repositories filled with impressive first commits and very little evidence that anybody came back.

Not necessarily because the people who created them were dishonest or incapable. Simply because starting a project is exciting, while maintaining one is often boring.

And AI doesn't eliminate that difference.

It can help write the patch, generate tests, investigate a crash, or update dependencies. It can make maintenance dramatically easier. Eventually, agents may handle much of that work autonomously.

But someone still has to decide that the work matters. Someone has to keep the system running, pay for what it needs, establish standards, and take responsibility when the automation fails.

The problem isn't just writing the code.

It's ensuring that someone continues to care about the code.

I think this changes how we should understand competitive advantage in software.

For a long time, technical difficulty provided a natural barrier. A sophisticated product was difficult to recreate because implementing it required knowledge, time, and resources.

As AI reduces those barriers, the mere existence of a feature becomes less impressive.

What matters increasingly is everything accumulated around that feature - the judgment behind decisions, the understanding of users, the reliability of releases, the willingness to fix unglamorous problems, and the discipline to continue improving something long after it stops feeling new.

Perhaps this is why I find the current obsession with how quickly something can be built a little misleading.

We see someone recreate an application in a few hours and immediately begin questioning the value of the original.

But we've only compared the cost of producing two versions of a product at one particular moment.

We haven't compared the cost of operating them for the next five years.

We haven't compared the number of problems solved, users supported, or difficult decisions made along the way.

And we haven't compared which developer will still be there when something inevitably goes wrong.

The ability to create software is becoming abundant.

The willingness to take responsibility for it may remain much rarer.

And maybe that's where the real opportunity lies.

Not in proving that you can build something impressive in a weekend, but in proving that you can keep making it better for months and years.

Because in a world where anyone can copy what you've built, one of the hardest things to copy is the fact that you keep showing up.
